Every business we've worked with runs part of itself on a spreadsheet. Bookings, quotes, stock, job sheets, who's
on which site on Tuesday. Spreadsheets are brilliant for this, right up to the point where they aren't, and that
point is easy to miss because nothing breaks. Things just get slower and more fragile.

## The signs

- **The same information is typed in twice.** Once into the spreadsheet, once into the invoice, once into the
  email to the customer. Every copy is a chance to get it wrong.
- **One person is the process.** They know which tab is current, which colour means what, and which rows to ignore.
  When they're off, things wait or go wrong.
- **"Who's got the latest version?"** is a question anyone asks, ever.
- **Chasing is a job.** Someone spends part of each day asking people whether they've done the thing, because
  there's no way to see it.
- **Reports take an afternoon.** The numbers exist. Getting them into the shape the owner or the accountant wants is
  a monthly ritual.
- **Mistakes are found by customers.** A double booking, a missed job, a quote that was never followed up.

Two or three of these and the spreadsheet is costing more than it looks. Not in licence fees, but in hours and
in errors.

## What a small app does differently

The word "app" sounds bigger than it is. What we build for businesses is usually a handful of screens, built around
one process, that:

- **asks for each piece of information once** and uses it everywhere it's needed;
- **shows everyone the same, current picture**, on a phone on site or a laptop in the office;
- **moves things along without chasing**: the job appears on the right person's list, the reminder goes out, the
  customer gets the update;
- **produces the reports as a by-product** rather than a monthly job;
- **stops the mistakes it can stop**: no double bookings, no quote without a follow-up date, no job sheet without a
  signature.

It doesn't replace the accounting package or the email. It sits in the middle and does the bit the spreadsheet was
straining to do.

## Why not an off-the-shelf tool?

Often you should use one, and we'll say so. If your process is the same as a thousand other businesses', someone has
built the tool already. The case for custom is when your process is the thing that makes you good, and the tools on
the market keep asking you to change it to fit them.

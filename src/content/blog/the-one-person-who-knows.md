There's usually one. The office manager who knows which customers pay late, which jobs need the big van and which
supplier to ring when the usual one is out. The engineer who's the only one who can quote the complicated work.
The person whose two weeks in Spain the whole company braces for.

They're brilliant, and they're a risk. Not because they'll do anything wrong, but because what they know lives in
their head, and heads go on holiday, get ill, retire and take other jobs.

## How it happens

Nobody plans it. The business grows faster than its systems. Each new wrinkle (this customer needs a PO number,
that site needs a permit, these jobs get a Friday reminder) is handled by the person who noticed it. They remember.
It works. Five years on, the process is them.

The signs are familiar:

- Questions from everyone else go to one person, all day.
- "Ask Sam" is the documentation.
- Things slow down or go wrong when they're away, and they come back to a pile.
- They can't take on anything new, because they're the bottleneck for everything current.
- Training a new starter means sitting next to them for a month.

## Why software helps, and why it isn't the whole answer

Writing it all down helps, and it goes stale the week after. What works better is putting the rules where the work
happens. If the system knows that this customer needs a PO number, it asks for one. If it knows Friday jobs get a
reminder, it sends it. The knowledge is still Sam's. It's just no longer only in Sam.

That's most of what a small custom app does for a business: it takes the dozens of small rules and reminders one
person carries and makes them part of how the work flows. Sam stops answering the same questions and starts doing
the things only Sam can do.

It also makes the person more valuable, not less. The best moment in these projects is watching the person who was
the bottleneck design the system that replaces the boring half of their job. They know every edge case. They're
the specification.

## Starting small

You don't fix this in one go. Pick the one question that person is asked most often, and the one mistake that
happens most when they're away. Build the small thing that answers the question and prevents the mistake. See how
much lighter it feels. Then pick the next one.
